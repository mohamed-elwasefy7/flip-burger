# Chapter 08 — Core Screens

*Sections 34–38 · The hero, the way people move, and the product page — the three surfaces where the experience is actually won or lost.*

---

## 34. The Hero Experience

### 34.1 The front door's job description

FLIP has no physical entrance; the hero carries everything a real doorway, host stand, and first waft of grill smoke would carry. In the **first three seconds** — roughly the attention a social-native visitor extends to a tapped link before bouncing — the hero must communicate three things, in this order:

1. **This is FLIP** — the wordmark and the flame-red-on-black relationship, instantly recognizable to anyone who has seen the brand's social content (the promise-payoff contract, Chapter 2, Section 7.3).
2. **This is fire, not another burger app** — real flame imagery, warm darkness, oversized type.
3. **There is somewhere to go** — a visible scroll cue and a clear primary action.

**Why order matters:** recognition before mood, mood before action. A visitor who acts without absorbing the mood converts once; a visitor who absorbs the mood becomes the repeat customer the loop depends on.

### 34.2 Anatomy of the hero

From back to front: the flame stage (the real-fire photographic layer — the single most important visual asset on the site) · rising ember ambience · the wordmark · a one-line location badge (الرياض · نار حقيقية / RIYADH · REAL FIRE — *pending approval*) · the slogan and support line · two CTAs · the scroll cue. Nothing else. Every element the hero does *not* contain — no carousel, no announcement bar, no social icons, no video background — is a deliberate subtraction defended by the Minimal principle (Chapter 3).

### 34.3 The ignition reveal

On first visit, the wordmark does not merely appear — it **ignites**: revealed as if lit from below, echoing the flip-of-the-patty founding gesture in the brand's very first animated frame. Two rules govern it: it plays **once per session only** — a returning visitor gets the wordmark instantly, because a repeated theatrical beat curdles into an obstacle (Chapter 2, Section 8.2) — and it must never delay interactivity; theater that costs usability fails the conflict-resolution order (Fast first, Chapter 3, Section 12).

### 34.4 The two-CTA law

The hero carries **exactly two** calls to action, permanently: one inviting exploration (استكشف المنيو / Explore Menu — the path for appetite still forming) and one shortcutting to order (اطلب الآن / Order Now — the path for the customer who already knows). Never a third. **Why:** a hero with three-plus CTAs is a brand admitting it doesn't know what it wants the visitor to do; FLIP always knows. Campaigns express themselves *inside* the menu (badges, the ladder), never by crowding the front door — this precedent is already ruled in Chapter 3, Section 12.3.

### 34.5 The scroll cue

A single ember that *falls* while the ambient embers *rise* — the inversion quietly points downward without an arrow or an instruction. The cue matters more than it looks: the hero is full-viewport, and a first-time visitor must never wonder whether there *is* anything below.

## 35. Navigation & Gesture System

### 35.1 The one true gesture

Navigation is **vertical, continuous scrolling** — one gesture, the one that never fails on any device, in either hand, at any competence level. The customer walks a single path through the rooms (Chapter 7); they never branch into sub-pages, never open hamburger menus to find food, never leave the path to see a product. **Why one path beats a menu tree:** every navigational decision demanded of the customer ("which section? which page?") is appetite-decay time; the single path replaces decisions with momentum.

### 35.2 The gesture contract

| Gesture | Does | Never does |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical scroll | Everything — the entire journey | Gets hijacked, snapped, or slowed by the site |
| Horizontal swipe | One job: browsing the category chips row | Hides products in carousels — content behind a swipe is content that doesn't sell |
| Tap | Selects, opens, orders | Requires precision — every target is comfortably thumb-sized (Chapter 10, Section 47) |
| Swipe-down | Closes the order sheet (with a visible handle) | Anything surprising |
| Long-press | Optional shortcut to quick actions on a product | Is ever the *only* way to reach anything (accessibility law, Chapter 10) |

**The no-hijack law:** the site never takes the scroll wheel from the customer — no scroll-jacking, no forced snap-points on desktop, no "cinematic" sequences that hold the page hostage. Smoothness is added *feel*; control stays with the thumb. A customer who feels the page resisting them experiences the brand resisting them.

### 35.3 The category chips

A single sticky row of category chips serves as both **map and teleport**: it always shows where the customer is (the active chip follows the scroll) and offers a one-tap jump for the decided customer (الليلي persona, Chapter 1) who came straight for the Party Box. The chips are the only navigational chrome in the whole experience — one row carrying the entire wayfinding load, which is exactly why it earns its permanent screen presence.

## 36. Progress & Transitions

### 36.1 The heat-line: progress as story

Scroll progress renders as a thin heat-gradient line at the viewport's top edge — red rising through yellow and back — so that *how far you are* and *how hot it gets* are the same signal (the one signature pattern, Chapter 4, Section 18.3). The gradient's yellow crest passes as the customer crosses the feast chapter: the menu's narrative arc, drawn in two pixels. **Why an atmospheric progress bar instead of a plain one:** a long single-path menu needs an "am I lost?" answer, but a bare utility bar would be the first cold, document-world element on the page; the heat-line answers the question *in the brand's own voice*.

### 36.2 Transition grammar

Transition weight must match narrative distance — the size of the visual event should equal the size of the story step:

| Step | Transition | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Within a room (product to product) | Light, quick reveals | Small story step, small event |
| Between rooms (category to category) | Ambient crossfade — tone, glow, and ember density hand over; the next room's ghost title pre-announces at the edge | A *felt* room change without a hard cut, preserving the one-continuous-space illusion |
| Into the order sheet | Rises over a dimmed stage | The counter appears *in front of* the room, not instead of it — context stays visible |
| Language switch | A brief full veil | The one legitimately violent DOM moment (the whole page re-renders and mirrors direction) hidden behind a deliberate blink — a page *turn*, not a page *glitch* |

### 36.3 Two transition laws

**Exits are always faster than entries.** Arriving deserves a considered beat; leaving must feel instant — lingering on the way out reads as the site clinging. **Nothing blocks input except the opening curtain** — which is hard-capped and skipped for returners (Chapter 2, Section 8.2). Every other moment of the experience, the customer can act.

## 37. Product Page Experience

### 37.1 One reading order, forever

Every product page presents in the same fixed order: **image → name → supporting detail (subtitle, ingredients, heat, meta) → price (when present) → action.** No exceptions per product, no seasonal rearrangements. **Why rigidity is a feature:** consistency compounds — by the third product, the customer's eye navigates on autopilot, and autopilot browsing is *faster appetite accumulation*. Every rearrangement would force re-learning, and re-learning is friction spent on nothing.

**Why this specific order:** craving is visual before it is verbal (image first); identity before detail (the name is the hook, the subtitle is the reel); **the CTA arrives last** because a button that appears before desire exists reads as pushy — the customer should already want the item by the time the button enters view, making the tap feel like *their* idea. That felt-ownership is the conversion.

### 37.2 The self-containment law

Everything needed to decide lives on the product's own stage: what it is, what's in it, how hot it is, what it costs (when pricing is live). The customer never scrolls elsewhere to compare or verify — the moment two products must be held in working memory across screens, decision fatigue begins, and fatigue resolves as "close the tab," not "pick one" (Chapter 2, Section 9.1).

### 37.3 The additive-field law

A product page must feel complete at **every** level of data richness. The structural skeleton is image + name + action; every other field — subtitle, description, ingredients, calories, prep time, heat level, badges, price — is *additive*: present, it enriches; absent, it leaves no hole, no empty label, no gap. **Why this is a storytelling law and not just an engineering nicety:** the menu grows and changes (new items launch with partial data; prices arrive later — pricing is currently intentionally absent awaiting final data). A layout that only looks right fully-populated punishes the brand for evolving, and punishes the sparse product by making it look broken rather than simply *simple*.

### 37.4 The stage, not the card

A FLIP product is presented as a **spotlight stage** (ghost type behind, the food floating lit in front — Chapter 4's materials and Chapter 6's photography meeting), never as a card in a grid of equals. One product owns the viewport at a time on mobile. The single exception is the drinks grid — a deliberate, narrative-driven exception (Chapter 7, Section 32.1), not a precedent. Grids inform; stages seduce; FLIP sells with the second.

## 38. Price, Badges & Trust

### 38.1 Price: confirming, never persuading

Price appears **after** image and name in the reading order — positioned as confirming information for a customer already convinced, never as a competing first impression. Presentation rules: plain round numbers in tabular figures, no ".95" psychological pricing — **why:** charm pricing signals *bargain*, and a premium cloud brand (Chapter 1, value #3) claiming bargain identity dissolves its own price justification. Round numbers read as confidence: this is what it costs, because it's worth it. When prices go live (the data slot exists, deliberately empty today), they slot into the established position with zero redesign — layout was built price-ready from day one.

### 38.2 Badges: scarce and true

Three badges exist — جديد / New, حار / Spicy (superseded visually by the heat ladder where levels are set), مميز / Featured — and their power is inversely proportional to their frequency:

| Badge | Meaning | Discipline |
|---|---|---|
| جديد | Genuinely recently launched | Auto-expires from the data after its honesty window (~30 days); a stale "new" is a small lie compounding daily |
| مميز | The house's genuine pick | A *few* items at any time; featured-everything is featured-nothing |
| Heat indicators | Kitchen-true ladder position | Never inflated for drama (Chapter 7, Section 33.4) |

**Never two endorsement badges on one item** — stacking "featured" logic dilutes both into generic "trust me" noise (the full psychology in Chapter 9, Section 39).

### 38.3 Trust: accumulated in details

Product-page trust is not a testimonial widget — it is the *accumulation of verifiable honesty*: photography that matches what the box delivers (the promise audit, Chapter 2, Section 10.1), the cross-section shot proving the build, ingredients stated plainly, calories and prep time offered without being forced, unavailable items saying so honestly instead of hiding. Each detail is small; together they produce the quiet conclusion that converts and retains: *this brand isn't lying to me.* On a screen where the customer can't smell or touch anything, accumulated honesty is the only trust there is.
