# Chapter 06 — Photography Bible

*Sections 23–28 · The most granular chapter in this Bible — because photography is FLIP's interior design. There is no dining room; the photograph is the only room the customer ever stands in.*

---

## 23. Camera, Lens & Composition

### 23.1 The default hero angle

The standard burger hero is shot **low — roughly 10–15° above the table plane**, looking slightly up at the stack. **Why:** a low angle makes a burger look tall, architectural, imposing — the stack *is* the story. A straight-down overhead flattens a burger into an anonymous circle and hides exactly the layering that triggers craving. Overhead framing is reserved for foods whose story is *spread*, not *height* — party boxes and cascading fries.

### 23.2 Lens character

The look is a **mid-telephoto macro perspective** (visually equivalent to roughly 85–100mm): compressed background, honest proportions, the food sharp edge-to-edge with only the environment beyond it falling soft. **Why not wide:** wide lenses at close range distort — buns bulge, proportions cartoon — the exact opposite of the confident, real-object presence FLIP needs. **Why not razor-thin focus on the food itself:** a burger with a blurry back edge reads as a camera trick; FLIP's product is fully sharp because the product can afford to be seen completely.

### 23.3 Composition law

- The product occupies **at least 60% of the frame's visual weight**, positioned for generous negative space on one side — that space is not empty; it is where ambient glow and ghost typography live in the final composed page (Chapter 8, Section 37). Photography and layout are designed *together*; a photo that fills its frame edge-to-edge leaves the page nowhere to breathe.
- One product per frame in menu photography. Group compositions belong to Party Box (Section 26) and campaign work only.
- Camera height, product scale, and backdrop remain consistent across every SKU in a category — the menu must read as **one shoot**, even when sessions are months apart. Consistency is what makes a menu look like a brand instead of a folder of photos.

## 24. Lighting Recipes

### 24.1 The master recipe

Every FLIP food photograph is lit by the same philosophy: **one hard warm key, deep shadows, no fill worth noticing.** The standard setup:

- **Key:** one hard light source, positioned 45° behind-and-to-the-side of the product (side-back), gelled warm — the visual temperature of flame (think 3200K territory). Hard, not softboxed: hard light carves char texture, seed detail, and cheese gloss; soft light flattens them into catalog blandness.
- **Shadow side:** black flags, not fill cards. The shadow side of a FLIP burger goes genuinely dark — that darkness is the black ladder (Chapter 4) existing inside the photograph itself, and it is what welds the photo to the interface around it.
- **Rescue bounce:** one small cream card, used only to lift a logo or label on packaging into legibility — never to open up the food's shadows.
- **Steam/backlight:** steam and smoke read only against darkness with light *behind* them — the side-back key does double duty here.

**Why one recipe for everything:** the light *is* the location. A cloud brand has no room; the consistent warm-key-deep-shadow signature is the room. Two lighting styles would be two restaurants.

### 24.2 Per-family adjustments

The master recipe flexes by exactly one parameter per category — never by changing the philosophy:

| Family | Adjustment | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Burgers | None — the master recipe at full contrast | The flagship defines the standard |
| Fried items (appetizers, future chicken) | Key raised slightly for stronger top highlights | Crunch is communicated by glinting texture; fried food sells on sparkle more than char |
| Party Box | Broader key coverage for the spread; same warmth | A wide scene needs even heat, but must never drift toward flat catalog lighting |
| Drinks | Backlight becomes primary; condensation is the subject | A cold can shot warm: the *light* stays fire-toned even when the product is chilled — the blue prohibition (Chapter 5) applies fully to photography |
| Desserts [FUTURE] | Softest permitted variant of the key | The reward beat reads gentler — but still warm, still shadowed, still FLIP |

### 24.3 The banned list

No ring lights. No flat frontal softboxes. No HDR tone-flattening. No cool or neutral white balance. No daylight-through-window lifestyle looks. Each of these produces competent food photography for *some* brand — and instantly the wrong photography for this one.

## 25. Styling & Technique — Fire, Steam, Cheese, Sauce, Stacking

### 25.1 The styling truth rule

**Everything in frame must be edible and real.** Real char from real contact with heat, real melted cheese, real sauce. No motor-oil syrup, no glue-thickened dairy, no cotton-wool smoke, no hidden armatures beyond basic food-safe stacking supports. **Why this is a hard line here when the whole industry bends it:** FLIP's entire brand rests on one falsifiable claim — *real fire*. The audience is a generation trained by exposé content to spot faked food styling; a single caught fake costs more trust than every perfect shot ever earned. Honest styling is not idealism — it is risk management for the brand's only load-bearing claim.

### 25.2 Steam

Steam is captured **live off genuinely hot food**, encouraged by a cool ambient room (temperature differential makes steam visible), read against the dark background via backlight. Never added digitally: real steam moves organically and non-repeating in a way the eye recognizes as true, and its presence quietly certifies that the food was actually hot when photographed — which is the entire promise of a delivery brand.

### 25.3 Fire in frame

Open flame behind or beside a product is photographed as a **separate controlled element** — a real flame pass composited only when geometry and color temperature genuinely match the product exposure. The test for an acceptable composite: the flame's light direction must agree with the key light's story. A flame camera-left with highlights camera-right reads instantly as fake — and a fake flame at FLIP is a contradiction of value #1 in public.

### 25.4 The cheese pull

The signature social shot, engineered rather than hoped-for: cheese at full melt, the lift executed in one continuous motion, shot at burst speed, the *stretch* backlit so its strands read against darkness. The pull must look like it happened at a table, not on a set — hands in frame are welcome (Section 26), gloves and tweezers are not. Reserved for hero products; a cheese pull on every item would be the yellow problem (Chapter 5, Section 21.5) in photographic form — spectacle spent into meaninglessness.

### 25.5 Sauce

Sauce is photographed **mid-event**: a drip frozen on the patty's edge, a swipe caught in motion — never a static pre-pooled puddle. Drips are placed on the shadow-side edge where they catch the key light as a highlight line. **Why mid-event:** stillness reads as "styled hours ago"; motion reads as "assembled seconds ago." A delivery brand sells the moment of assembly, not the moment of the photoshoot.

### 25.6 Stack architecture

The burger build is photographed in its honest assembly order, with structural discipline: patty char face toward camera, cheese draped so its melt-edge shows, produce placed for color rhythm (never a lettuce wall hiding the patty), crown tilted only as far as a real bun sits. Height may be *composed* — every element angled to present its best face — but never *falsified*: no double-stacking a single-patty product, no hidden risers creating impossible height. The cross-section shot (Section 26) exists precisely to prove the build honest, and every hero shot must survive comparison with its own cross-section.

## 26. Category Shot Recipes

Each category's recipe serves its narrative role (Chapter 7). Live categories first, future categories planned so they are never designed under launch pressure:

| Category | Primary recipe | Supporting shots | Distinct treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Burgers** | ¾ view, low angle, full stack, steam live | Cheese-pull macro (hero SKUs only) · cross-section (one per patty base — the honesty shot) | Warmest light, deepest shadows, maximum char detail |
| **Appetizers / Sides** | 45° cascade — fries mid-tumble, rings leaning, motion implied | Overhead spread for shareables | Faster, crackling energy; more sparkle, less brood |
| **Party Box** | Top-down full spread, **hands reaching in** | Lid-open reveal (the unboxing promise, Chapter 2, Section 10) | The *only* category with people in frame — hands sell scale and occasion; faces never appear (the food is the star, and faces date a campaign instantly) |
| **Drinks** | Straight-on, backlit condensation, warm-lit despite cold product | One chapter-intro hero: condensation on pure darkness | Calm, minimal — the visual exhale |
| **Packaging** | The box as product: low ¾ like a burger, logo catching the key | Box-open steam reveal; courier-bag brand shot | The box is photographed with the same reverence as food — it *is* the storefront |
| **Chicken [FUTURE]** | Inherits the burger recipe wholesale | Crispy-texture macro replaces cheese pull as the signature | Highlights lifted for crunch; same family, different protein — not a sub-brand |
| **Loaded Fries [FUTURE]** | 45° side view showing base-to-topping strata | Topping-avalanche detail | Treated as a *build* (burger family), never as a garnished side |
| **Desserts [FUTURE]** | Close macro, single angle, softest permitted key | Cross-section for layered items | The reward beat: gentle, indulgent, still warm and dark |
| **Kids [FUTURE]** | Simple, bright-end-of-warm, single item, zero clutter | — | Approachable without leaving the brand's world; explicitly **no** heat-ladder or dare styling anywhere near children's items |

## 27. Editing, Retouching, Compression & Ratios

### 27.1 The retouching philosophy

**Enhance what happened; never fabricate what didn't.**

| Permitted | Banned |
|---|---|
| Exposure, contrast, warmth grading toward the brand look | Adding steam, char, cheese, or sauce that wasn't captured |
| Selective saturation lift on the food itself | Compositing elements from different products |
| Removing stray crumbs, fingerprints on packaging, rig traces | Reshaping the food's actual geometry |
| Grain unification across a shoot | **Any AI generation or AI "enhancement" of the food itself — absolute, no exceptions** (Chapter 11, Section 50 governs the narrow non-food exceptions) |

The grade always moves toward the palette (Chapter 5): warm blacks stay warm, highlights stay flame-toned, nothing drifts cool in the shadows — cool shadows are the most common grading drift and the most corrosive to the fire-world.

### 27.2 Aspect ratios

Ratios are chosen by the *page*, not by the camera: burgers and appetizers in portrait 4:5 (or 1:1), because the mobile product stage is portrait-dominant and a stack is vertical; party boxes 1:1 or 4:3 for the top-down spread; drinks 1:1 centered. Existing assets in a near-3:4 portrait remain valid — the pipeline preserves them — and new shoots standardize going forward. **The crop rule:** compose final crops *at the shoot*. The production pipeline only ever downscales — it never crops, filters, or upscales — so a composition error at the shoot is permanent. This pipeline austerity is deliberate: it makes the photographer, not a build script, the last authority on composition.

### 27.3 Delivery & compression

Modern-format-first delivery (AVIF, then WebP, then JPEG fallback), multiple width tiers so a phone never downloads a monitor's image, and a strict average per-image weight budget (Chapter 11, Section 48). **Why compression is a photography topic and not just an engineering one:** an image that adds a second of load time has already lost its viewer before it renders — the most beautiful photograph at the wrong weight is a worse photograph, in business terms, than a good one at the right weight.

## 28. Social Motion Masters

### 28.1 The four loops

Four short motion pieces constitute FLIP's core social ammunition — each about two seconds, each loopable, shot as vertical 9:16 masters with square crops derived:

| Loop | Content | Why this one |
|---|---|---|
| **The Flip** | The patty turn in slow motion, char face revealing | The brand's founding gesture (Chapter 1) made literal — the single most on-brand seconds of footage possible |
| **The Stretch** | Cheese pull rising, backlit, strands against darkness | The category's universal craving trigger, in FLIP's own lighting signature |
| **The Lick** | Flame surging behind a patty on the grill | The "real fire" claim as evidence, not copy |
| **The Reveal** | Box lid opening, steam escaping toward camera | Connects the digital promise to the physical unboxing — the journey's loop point (Chapter 2, Section 10) in two seconds |

### 28.2 Motion-capture rules

The photography bible applies unreduced to motion: same warm key, same deep shadows, same honesty rules — slow motion is the only added ingredient. Loops must cut seamlessly (their power on social is the hypnotic repeat), carry no burned-in text (captions are added per-platform in the platform's native voice), and carry no music dependency (they must work silent — most feeds are). Sound design for these loops lives with the social platforms' conventions, not the website (Chapter 10, Section 46).

### 28.3 The library discipline

Every shoot produces stills *and* at least one motion master per hero product, cataloged so campaigns never wait on a reshoot. The four loops above are evergreen; campaign-specific motion (challenge content, seasonal drops) builds on top of them rather than replacing them — the evergreen library is the brand's visual savings account.
